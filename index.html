Who actually competes in this space, and on what?

Grouping the market by product positioning and customer segment surfaces three competitive tiers. Comprehensive Corporate Intelligence Platforms compete most directly on breadth of Indian corporate data. Startup & Private-Market Intelligence Platforms compete on adjacent private-market and startup intelligence. A third tier of KYC/KYB and compliance-workflow platforms competes less on data depth and more on how tightly they sit inside a customer's existing process.

Positioning reflects stated product focus from the industry research, not measured market share.
The strategic observation from the research

The market is moving from "who has the most data?" to "who can make the data most useful within the customer's workflow?"

That's the open quadrant above — and it's the quadrant this project's concept (Section 07) is aimed at.

It's largely open because the incumbents' own business model works against it: most data providers monetise on API calls or bulk exports, which rewards handing over more raw data — not building an opinionated, filtered layer that gives users less to sift through.

05The Opportunity

Where could the industry go next?

The bigger opportunity is making better use of data that already exists, rather than collecting more of it. Mapped against the find → understand → prioritise → act journey from Section 04, three directions stood out.

Opportunity 01

Easier to find

From searching for information to discovering relevant information — surfacing signals based on context, geography, industry.

Opportunity 02

Easier to understand

From isolated data points to connected context — bringing company, financial, ownership and risk data together.

Opportunity 03

Easier to prioritise

From understanding information to knowing what deserves attention — turning a broad set of information into a short, ranked list worth acting on.

Comparing the three

Weighing potential impact against how hard each would be to build. Prioritise scores highest on impact — and it's the one competitors have solved for least.

The chosen direction

Two ways to build "easier to prioritise"

Having picked Opportunity 03, there are two honest routes to get there.

Option A

Keep expanding the data catalogue

Strengths

Builds on existing capability, expands coverage, lower disruption to the current product.

Limitations

Rising data costs, diminishing differentiation as data commoditises, exposure to generic AI search & summarisation.

Option B

Build a workflow intelligence layer on top of the data

Strengths

Moves closer to the customer's core workflow, stronger differentiation, higher switching costs, value-based pricing.

Limitations

Requires deeper workflow understanding, more integration complexity, and stronger trust & explainability.

The choice: Option B

Keep the existing data as the foundation. Build the prioritisation layer on top of it — that layer is where the differentiation comes from.

07Product UX & Interface Features

What the interface actually does for Sales & BD.

Section 06 set the approach. This section is about the interface itself — the three UX commitments that decide whether a ranked list is trusted and acted on: explainable scores, primary-source citation, and one-click action.

Three interface features
Feature 01

Explainable match scores

Every ranking criterion is visible and traceable — each score links back to the primary source entries that produced it (MCA filings, GST records, registry entries), so a user can see why a company ranks where it does.

Feature 02

Primary source citation

Direct drill-down links open the original regulatory filing — no black-box AI summaries standing in for the record. If a claim can't be traced to a source, it isn't shown as fact.

Feature 03

One-click action triggers

Quick-action buttons let Sales & BD teams export a lead list or initiate outreach from the ranked view itself — the decision and the next step live in the same place.

Illustrative wireframe — not built functionality. Match %, signal tags, and reasoning lines are how the concept could make its logic visible to the user.

Explainable, not black-box: why this matters for compliance buyers

In RegTech and enterprise sales, a black-box recommendation is a hard sell — compliance teams need to know why, not just what. Financial and regulatory intelligence has to be verifiable, not just plausible. Two design commitments follow from that: every match score comes with an audit trail back to its primary source — an MCA filing, GST record, registry entry, or disclosure, not a generated summary — and confidence is shown as a score rather than hidden behind confident-sounding language. If a claim can't be traced to a source, it isn't shown as fact. This isn't hypothetical caution: RBI's June 2026 draft guidance on Model Risk Management already proposes mandatory "kill switch" capability and board-level accountability for AI-driven decisions at banks and NBFCs — any concept in this space needs to be built for that world from day one.

Source: RBI Draft Guidance on Regulatory Principles for Model Risk Management, June 2026, reported in Economic Times & ThePrint.
This pattern is already paying off elsewhere in Indian BFSI

The expansion path in Card 03 isn't speculative — decision-layer automation is already showing measurable returns in adjacent workflows: a ₹1,000 crore NBFC cut underwriting time 75% with 3–4x productivity gains; Aditya Birla Finance cut underwriting time 70%; IIFL Finance moved policy deployment from four weeks to minutes, with 75% lower delinquency.

Sources: LendingIQ NBFC case study, 2025 · Tartan × Aditya Birla Finance case study, 2025 · FinBox × IIFL Finance case study, 2026.

The interface has one job: make the reasoning visible, then make the next step one click away.

09From Strategy To Execution

Start small. Learn fast. Scale what works.

A strategy is only useful if it can survive contact with reality — so the initial execution follows a test → learn → improve → scale cycle.

Phase 1 · Weeks 1–4

Validate

Speak with target users, map their workflow, test the concept with realistic scenarios. Is this a real pain?

Phase 2 · Weeks 5–8

Pilot

Test one focused use case with design partners and measure where users find value. Does it actually help?

Phase 3 · Weeks 9–12

Prove & expand

Measure business impact, document use cases, prepare for wider rollout. Can we show measurable value?

What to build first — MVP scope

The first release covers find → understand → prioritise only. The human still makes the call and takes the action.

Automate the work around the decision — not the decision itself.

This layered approach — automated scoring feeding into, not replacing, policy and human judgment — mirrors how lenders like CASHe already combine ML scoring with policy rules rather than one replacing the other.

10Measuring Success

If the goal is better decisions, how do we know we're helping?

Traditional metrics like logins or searches only tell us whether people are using the product — not whether it's creating value. So success is measured across three levels.

Level 1 · Adoption

Active users, repeat usage, feature adoption, frequency of use.

Level 2 · Workflow Impact

Time to shortlist, manual steps reduced, confidence in prioritisation.

Level 3 · Business Impact

Qualified opportunities generated, conversion, pipeline and revenue influenced.

The north star metric

Time to Qualified Opportunity

The time it takes a user to move from starting a search to identifying an opportunity worth pursuing. Proposed north star metric, to be validated during the pilot.

What "good" looks like, elsewhere

Mature workflow-automation deployments in Indian BFSI report turnaround-time cuts of 38–84% and ROI north of 300% within their first year — a useful outer bound for what Level 2 and Level 3 metrics could realistically reach once this moves past pilot.

Sources: IBM BPM for Indian Banks, VLink Info, Feb 2026 · Consumer Loan Origination Software case study, Servosys, Mar 2026.
11Risks, Trade-offs & What's Next

A strong strategy names its biggest assumptions — not just its plan.

Risk 01 · Trust

Will users act on it?

Make the reasoning visible — show why an opportunity is prioritised, not just that it is, so the user can sanity-check it themselves.

Risk 02 · Data Quality

Can it be trusted?

Bad inputs produce confident-sounding bad outputs. Focus on data freshness, source transparency, and the ability to verify any claim.

Risk 03 · User Habit Inertia

Will people change what they already do?

Users are already used to their current tools. Embed intelligence into existing workflows rather than forcing a separate destination.

Risk 04 · Over-Automation

Will users trust it — or resist it?

People push back if they feel judgement is being replaced. Position this as decision support — the human stays accountable for the final call. RBI's draft Model Risk Management guidance already requires exactly this kind of human-in-the-loop accountability for banks and NBFCs.

Risk 05 · Competitive Convergence

Will adjacent players close the gap?

Workflow platforms could add intelligence; intelligence platforms could add workflow. The moat has to be the decision layer, not the data alone.
